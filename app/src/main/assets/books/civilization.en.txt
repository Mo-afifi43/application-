# Lights of Islamic Civilisation

== Knowledge as worship
The first word of the revelation was "Read". A religion that begins this way was bound to produce a civilisation of books. The Prophet ﷺ made the ransom of some prisoners of Badr the teaching of ten Muslim children to write, and he said that the scholars are the heirs of the prophets, and that the angels lower their wings for the seeker of knowledge in pleasure at what he does.
> يَرْفَعِ اللَّهُ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مِنكُمْ وَالَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْعِلْمَ دَرَجَاتٍ
~ Qur'an 58:11 — "Allah raises those who have believed among you and those who were given knowledge by degrees."
From the mosque of Madinah grew a culture in which learning was worship. Students travelled for months to hear a single hadith from the one who heard it from the one who heard it from the Prophet ﷺ — the isnad, a chain of names that made Muslim scholarship the most carefully documented in the ancient world. Every scholar gave his students an ijazah, a licence proving what he had transmitted and from whom.
Women taught alongside men. Aisha taught the companions; Umm al-Darda taught the caliph in the mosque of Damascus; Fatimah al-Fihri founded in Fez, in the year 859, what many consider the oldest continuously operating university in the world; Karimah al-Marwaziyyah was one of the most trusted transmitters of Sahih al-Bukhari, and great scholars travelled to Makkah to hear it from her.
Endowments (waqf) paid for schools, libraries, teachers and students, so that a poor boy from a village could rise to become a judge or a physician. Paper, learned from China and produced in Samarkand and Baghdad, made books cheap, and Cairo, Cordoba and Baghdad had libraries of hundreds of thousands of volumes when the largest libraries of Europe held a few hundred.
## Lesson
A civilisation is what a people do with the command "Read". Ours built libraries.

== The scholars of hadith
The Prophet ﷺ said: "Whoever lies about me deliberately, let him take his seat in the Fire." The community took this warning so seriously that it created an entire science to protect his words: the study of narrators, their honesty, their memory, whom they met and when, and the comparison of every chain of transmission with every other.
Muhammad ibn Isma'il al-Bukhari (194–256 AH) is its greatest name. Orphaned as a child, blessed with a prodigious memory, he travelled for sixteen years across Iraq, the Hijaz, Syria and Egypt, collecting some six hundred thousand narrations. From them he selected about seven thousand with the strictest conditions, praying two units and seeking Allah's guidance before recording each one. His Sahih became, after the Qur'an, the most authentic book of Islam.
His student Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj (204–261 AH) produced the second Sahih, admired for the elegance of its arrangement. Together they are "the two Sahihs", and a hadith found in both is described as "agreed upon". Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah compiled the four Sunan that, with the two Sahihs, form the six canonical books.
Later scholars served these books as their teachers had served the Prophet ﷺ. Al-Nawawi wrote the Riyad al-Salihin and the Forty Hadith; Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani spent twenty-five years writing Fath al-Bari, the commentary on Bukhari that no scholar since has been able to do without.
## Lesson
Truth is preserved by people who love it enough to verify it.

== The four Imams
Four scholars gave their names to the schools of law that the vast majority of Muslims follow to this day. They never met as a group, but they honoured one another, and their differences were the mercy the Prophet ﷺ promised the nation.
## Abu Hanifah (80–150 AH)
A silk merchant of Kufa of Persian origin, he was famous for his sharp reasoning and for training a circle of students to debate every question before he ruled on it. He refused the office of chief judge offered by the caliph, was imprisoned for it, and died in prison. He gave a third of his profits in charity and supported his students from his own wealth.
## Malik ibn Anas (93–179 AH)
The Imam of Madinah never left the city of the Prophet ﷺ. He would perform wudu, put on his best clothes and perfume before narrating hadith, out of respect for the Prophet's words. His Muwatta is the earliest surviving book of hadith and law. When the caliph Harun al-Rashid asked him to come to the palace to teach his sons, he replied: "Knowledge is sought; it does not come to people," and the princes came to sit in his circle with everyone else.
## Al-Shafi'i (150–204 AH)
Born in Gaza and raised as a poor orphan in Makkah, he memorised the Qur'an at seven and the Muwatta at ten, studied under Malik in Madinah and under the students of Abu Hanifah in Baghdad, and settled in Egypt. His al-Risalah founded the science of the principles of jurisprudence — how to derive law from the Qur'an and Sunnah. He said: "I never debated anyone except wishing that Allah would make the truth appear on his tongue."
## Ahmad ibn Hanbal (164–241 AH)
The Imam of Baghdad, a student of al-Shafi'i, who said of him: "I left Baghdad and did not leave behind a man more pious or more knowledgeable than Ahmad." His Musnad contains about thirty thousand hadith. When the caliph tried to force the scholars to declare that the Qur'an was created, Ahmad refused, was flogged and imprisoned for years, and did not change a word. His steadfastness saved the creed of the nation.
## Lesson
Four men, four cities, one Qur'an and one Sunnah — and the humility to say: "My opinion is correct but may be wrong, and the opinion of others is wrong but may be correct."

== The scientists and the House of Wisdom
In the early ninth century the Abbasid caliphs of Baghdad founded Bayt al-Hikmah, the House of Wisdom, where scholars of every faith translated the works of Greece, Persia and India into Arabic and then went beyond them. For five hundred years Arabic was the language of science.
Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi (about 780–850) wrote the book from whose title we take the word "algebra" and from whose name we take "algorithm". He introduced the Indian numerals to the Muslim world, from where they reached Europe as "Arabic numerals".
Ibn al-Haytham (965–1040) proved by experiment that we see because light enters the eye, not because rays leave it, and his Book of Optics laid down the method of testing a hypothesis by controlled experiment centuries before it was named the scientific method.
Ibn Sina (980–1037) wrote The Canon of Medicine, the medical textbook of both the Muslim world and Europe for six hundred years. Al-Zahrawi of Cordoba (936–1013) wrote the first illustrated surgical manual and invented dozens of instruments still recognisable today. Al-Biruni measured the radius of the earth with remarkable accuracy; Jabir ibn Hayyan established the laboratory methods of chemistry; and the astronomers of Maragha produced planetary models that later reached Copernicus.
Islamic cities built hospitals — bimaristans — that treated everyone free of charge, with separate wards, pharmacies, medical schools and even music for the mentally ill. The hospital of Ahmad ibn Tulun in Cairo, founded in 872, kept the patient's belongings safe and gave him a new set of clothes and money when he was discharged.
## Lesson
Faith and science were never enemies in Islam. The scientists prayed, and the mosques housed the libraries.

== Al-Andalus and the endowments
For nearly eight centuries, Muslim Spain was a beacon of learning. In the tenth century Cordoba had paved and lit streets, running water, public baths and, in the library of the caliph al-Hakam II, some four hundred thousand books, when the great library of a European monastery held a few hundred. Scholars of the three faiths studied side by side, and students came from across Europe to learn.
From al-Andalus came Ibn Rushd, the philosopher and judge whose commentaries on Aristotle shaped European thought; Ibn Hazm, the jurist and poet; al-Zahrawi the surgeon; and Ibn Zuhr the physician. In Toledo, after the city changed hands, the translators turned Arabic books into Latin, and through them Europe received its own lost heritage together with the new science of the Muslims.
The Alhambra of Granada, with its calligraphy of "There is no victor but Allah" carved a thousand times into its walls, still stands as a witness to what beauty in the service of faith can produce.
## The waqf
Behind every school, hospital, fountain and bridge of Islamic civilisation stood the waqf: an endowment given for the sake of Allah whose income serves a good purpose forever. There were endowments for feeding travellers, for the dowries of poor brides, for replacing broken pots so that servants would not be beaten, for milk for orphans, for stray cats, and for the maintenance of city walls. The Prophet ﷺ had said that when a person dies, his deeds end except three: a continuing charity, beneficial knowledge, and a righteous child who prays for him.
> إِذَا مَاتَ ابْنُ آدَمَ انْقَطَعَ عَمَلُهُ إِلاَّ مِنْ ثَلاثٍ: صَدَقَةٍ جَارِيَةٍ، أَوْ عِلْمٍ يُنْتَفَعُ بِهِ، أَوْ وَلَدٍ صَالِحٍ يَدْعُو لَهُ.
~ Muslim — "When the son of Adam dies, his deeds end except three: a continuing charity, knowledge that benefits, or a righteous child who prays for him."
## Lesson
Civilisation is not only what we build, but what we leave behind that continues to benefit others after us. Every Muslim can plant a waqf — a tree, a book, a well, a student — and continue earning after death.
